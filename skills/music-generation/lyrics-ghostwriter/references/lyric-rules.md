## 1. Voice and emotional truth

Write from inside the speaker's experience. Use plain vocabulary, contractions, physical sensation, action, and speech that sounds like something this person would say. Let the listener infer the larger condition from the situation, while allowing the speaker to state what hurts.

Hold incompatible truths together without explaining them away. In the reference songs, the speaker wants company and pushes it away; needs usefulness and longs to be wanted without it; misses a mentor and refuses the relationship; escapes danger and loses what made survival worth it. Make both sides credible through the song.

Distinguish the speaker's fear from a fact. Song 3 admits the ship might always have turned that way; the speaker nevertheless feels responsible. Preserve such uncertainty when the brief calls for it.

Match the emotional movement to the request. Allow a boundary to bring relief (song 4), a struggle to become exhaustion (song 5), or nostalgia to coexist with anger (song 9). Do not default every ending to triumph, collapse, a lesson, or a clever question.

Use profanity as part of voice and pressure. It can intensify a bridge or final chorus and can occur several times when that is the speaker's speech. Do not sprinkle it into weak lines to simulate feeling. Do not censor supplied mature themes or add reassurance inside the song.

## 2. Connected imagery and direct speech

Choose images for what they reveal or do in this song. A household object is not emotional evidence on its own, and a surreal phrase is not depth on its own. Give the image a legible physical or emotional relationship to the surrounding lines.

Use either, or a purposeful combination, of these approaches:

- **Inhabited scenes:** Follow a consequential action, perception, hesitation, or failed attempt. In song 1, typing and erasing an invitation reveals a conflict through a sequence. In song 9, a name rises with good news before an empty chair interrupts the reflex.
- **Sustained metaphor worlds:** Give the image world stable relationships. In song 3, signals, navigation, maintenance, fatigue, and passing ships all express dependence on usefulness. In songs 7–8, rooms, fog, pursuit, and contaminated refuges express recurring fear and loss.

Make adjacent lines belong to the same experience. Let an image develop or return with changed meaning. Do not move through mouths, crowns, teeth, graves, gods, and skies merely to keep each line figurative.

Keep direct speech available throughout the song. Lines such as “I want you close. I want you gone.” or “I miss you, and I stay the fuck away.” carry the contradiction without needing symbolic decoration. “I'm scared of not being scared” names a change the preceding song has earned. These are core techniques, not reluctant exceptions.

Use scene-setting and connective lines when they give the listener a place to stand. Do not force every line to carry a new revelation. Avoid clinical analysis, explanatory backstory dumps, and self-help generalizations; distinguish them from a plain emotional admission.

Use surreal images when the context makes them coherent. The broken sun and unlit ceiling in song 1 contrast an in-game victory with the unchanged room. Their connection matters more than their novelty.

Do not ban or prescribe any prop from these examples. Phones, photos, empty chairs, fog, rooms, and screens can work, but copying them into every brief does not reproduce the style. The user's rejection of a draft does not prove that each noun or technique in it is forbidden.

## 3. Musicality and phrasing

Build phrases a singer can speak and sustain in a natural voice. Prefer simple sentence shapes, audible stressed words, and clear places to breathe. Use full stops, fragments, repeated phrases, and interruptions when they shape delivery or thought.

Use roughly 6–10 syllables as a common starting range for compact verse lines, not a rule or pass/fail test. The corpus includes shorter fragments and longer lines in verses, choruses, and bridges. Keep a long line when its natural stress and phrase grouping serve the moment; cut it when it forces rushed delivery or obscures the emotion.

For an 80 BPM half-time arrangement, leave room for held words and silence. A phrase can occupy more than one bar. Do not equate a short printed line with good rhythm, or an uneven line with failure. Do not use unmetered prose as a shortcut to emotional collapse.

Check each line beside its neighbors for stress, breath, and momentum. Avoid adding an adjective, twisting word order, or inventing an image just to complete a meter or rhyme. Avoid filling every line with clipped opposites just to depict instability.

Use end rhyme, slant rhyme, internal sound, repeated syntax, and unrhymed speech as the song needs. Song 1 supports connected rhymes; songs 4 and 9 support speech-led phrasing. Do not force couplets across a whole song. Do not reject a useful rhyme merely because it is exact.

Let repetition have a job: recognition, bargaining, obsession, a failed attempt to believe something, or a musical return. “I know this place. I know this place.” belongs to recognition that cannot provide escape. Do not repeat an invented slogan just to fill space.

For manic elements, distinguish intensity from pace. Show the convincing promise of a good day, its overcommitment, its cost, or a perception that will not settle. Do not assume the word requires rapid rap, a clinical diagnosis, or minute-by-minute mood switches.

## 4. Section movement

Choose a form that serves this song. Do not impose one default structure. The corpus includes eight-line and shorter verses, four-line and six-line choruses, a song with no pre-chorus, bridges with different phrase lengths, and final choruses that change the outcome.

**Verse 1:** Place the listener inside the current experience or reflex. Establish enough context for the images and voice to make sense. Avoid listing the brief's themes or staging generic symptoms.

**Pre-chorus:** Concentrate pressure or move the situation toward the chorus. It need not accelerate the singing. Reuse it when the same trap returns; change it when the situation develops. Omit it if the song does not need it.

**Chorus:** Express the conflict the verses make unavoidable. It can be a confession, demand, fear, question, unresolved contradiction, or downward admission. It does not need a title at the end, three images, four lines, or a punchline. Make it memorable through phrasing, emotional specificity, and return. Do not just reword the user's premise as a generic rhetorical question. Choose the title from the song if needed; do not build filler around a title chosen first.

**Verse 2:** Change the situation. Show a cost, an attempted correction that fails, a loss of refuge, or a deepening contradiction. Do not start the same introduction again with different props. Preserve rhythmic kinship without requiring matching line counts.

**Bridge:** Let the speaker say, ask, or do what the earlier sections have made difficult. Plain language can be the climax. A small action can also be the climax, as in the erased message in song 1. Do not require commands, shouting, a moral, or a twist. Let a longer phrase through if the music can hold it.

**Final chorus:** Keep what still matters, and change what the experience has changed. The corpus often turns fear into exhaustion, unwanted space into loneliness, or restrained longing into an unfiltered admission. A changed final chorus is encouraged when earned, not mandatory. Preserve an exact refrain if requested. Adding profanity alone is not a new emotional development, though it can express one.

Include no intros or outros. Ambient production does not require an extra lyric section. Do not append a neat saying to explain the song.

## 5. Reference-specific lessons

Use the full songs in approved-songs.md to calibrate these observations:

| 1: Fantasy and longing | Let the desired world and physical room coexist, then reveal what cannot cross between them. | A list of gaming nouns or a screen in every song. |
| 2: Push away / want close | Make contradictory needs produce commitments, withdrawal, harm, and loneliness. | Alternating “up” and “down” statements. |
| 3: Usefulness and belonging | Sustain a metaphor through work, failure, ambiguous evidence, and the wish for company without a role. | Nautical decoration or a tidy statement about self-worth. |
| 4: Refusing social performance | Build anger from repeated social injury; let leaving produce a physical release and ownership of time. | Insults, superiority, or a universal claim that solitude is best. |
| 5: Losing resistance | Change panic into familiarity and make that change frightening. | Louder symptoms in every section. |
| 6: Lost years / wanting relief | Make elapsed time physical; let accumulated restraint break into a demand for life. | A shock prop or a quota of profanity. |
| 7: Pursuit and recognition | Let an external threat become intimate through a recognizable bodily detail. | Horror imagery without a connection to this speaker. |
| 8: Survival without learning | Preserve the cost of escape; let a former refuge become part of the threat. | Defiant persistence or praise for resilience. |
| 9: Missing the former mentor | Let an old reflex meet present absence; hold longing and the justified boundary together. | Reconciliation, romance, or a replacement of the former mentor with a different person. |

## 6. Failure patterns

Reject these patterns during drafting:

- Decorative metaphor: a striking phrase that lacks a stable meaning or does no work in context.
- Prop substitution: switching a rejected photo for a mirror, empty room, or old shirt while keeping the failed construction.
- Disconnected clever lines: each line sounds quotable, but no experience develops across them.
- Premise paraphrase: the chorus restates the prompt without inhabiting its emotional cost.
- Forced closure: a neat aphorism, pun, lesson, or punchline where the speaker is unresolved.
- Mechanical musicality: identical syllable counts, obligatory rhymes, or fragment after fragment with no natural vocal phrase.
- Manufactured instability: arbitrary opposing claims or surreal bodily changes in place of a credible unstable experience.
- Borrowed authority: a new draft repeating the approved songs' signature lines, imagery, or endings as if they were a phrase bank.
- Escalation by volume alone: each verse adds only more darkness, more profanity, or louder declarations.
- Unasked resolution: adding hope, forgiveness, certainty, or defiance that contradicts the brief.

Use these as diagnostic tests, not word bans. Do not label an unreviewed assistant draft as an approved example or a confirmed rejection. The nine songs supplied by the user are the positive corpus.

## 7. Final review and revision

Before delivering, ask:

1. Can I identify the speaker's desire and the conflicting truth through the song, without an explanatory note?
2. Does each scene or image develop the same experience, and can a listener follow its connection?
3. Have I kept useful direct admissions instead of decorating them, and removed explanations that stop the song?
4. Can I phrase this at the requested pace with natural stress, held words, and room to breathe?
5. Does the chorus carry this speaker's conflict, and does it work as a sung return rather than a slogan?
6. Does verse 2 change something? Does the bridge earn its admission? Does the ending follow from what happened?
7. Have I avoided both copying the references and contradicting the craft they demonstrate?
8. Have I followed revision scope and exact-text requirements and counted the final deliverable?

Fix the failing pattern across the requested scope. Do not patch one rhyme when the whole image world is wrong. Do not rewrite an approved section to accommodate an unapproved idea. On a whole-draft rejection, read the whole reference corpus before trying a new approach; do not justify the old draft or make the user supply a line-by-line diagnosis.

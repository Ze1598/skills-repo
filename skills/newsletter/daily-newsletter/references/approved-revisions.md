## Ticket target: final user revision

### You’re rewarding the problem you want gone

A support manager introduced a target for tickets closed per week to reduce the backlog. When a scheduled data import failed, an engineer restarted it, checked that the data arrived, and closed the ticket.

The customer could continue working. The queue had one fewer item. The target gave the team a clear measure of progress, and the restart counted toward it.

Then the import failed on another run.

The engineer proposed investigating the recurring fault. A restart restored service, but it left the cause untouched. Finding and removing that cause would take time she could spend closing other tickets.

The manager told her to meet the weekly target before starting the investigation. Customers were waiting, and the backlog needed attention. She restarted the import, closed the ticket, and moved to the next request.

Each time the failure returned, the team repeated the fix. Each fix produced another closure. By the end of the week, the team met its closed ticket target. Yet, customers kept losing access to the data they needed, and engineers kept spending time on the same problem.

The problem had become a source of good performance.

Nobody needed to fake a result. Just restarting an import was an easy ticket. But the target gave the team credit for looking competent, not for delivering good solutions. And fixing the root cause would cost a lot more time to close just one ticket.

A target does more than describe progress. When you use it to judge performance and decide what people can work on, it becomes a rule for allocating their time. Work that improves the number gets priority. Work without visible value has to fight for permission.

That’s where the measure began to compete with its purpose. The manager wanted less work in the pile and dependable service at the same time. His instruction gave priority to rushing through individual tickets over fixing problems.

Before enforcing a target, ask what happens to someone’s performance record when their work clashes with your target. If preventing more easy tickets makes the person look worse, then you need to change what you’re evaluating.

## Delegation: final user revision

### You can’t delegate ownership and keep every decision

The delivery manager asked the tech lead to rescue a late data migration project. Before she could change scope, move engineers, or update the client, she needed his approval.

He wanted one person accountable for the recovery… but also wanted to control decisions that could affect the contract. Removing work could change what the client received. Moving an engineer could delay another stream. Warning the client could lose their confidence.

The lead reviewed the remaining work and sent him three decisions: drop a low-value report, move an engineer from another stream, and warn the client about a possible delay.

The manager spent most of the day in client calls. The decisions waited in his inbox. The team continued with the same scope and staffing, and the client received no warning.

At the next status review, the migration remained late. The lead was responsible for recovering it, but every change she proposed needed a decision from the manager.

He delegated the pressure and kept the control.

When you assign ownership, you’re asking someone to choose how to reach an outcome and adapt to changes. That requires authority over some of the decisions that shape the result. If all they can do is report the problem and request permission, you’ve given them responsibility for chasing decisions.

You remain the bottleneck.

For this tech lead, ownership needed boundaries for scope, staffing, cost, and client communication. She needed to know what she could change, which resources she could use, and what she could tell the client without asking. Decisions outside those limits would come back to the manager.

Set those boundaries when you assign the outcome. Within them, let the owner decide and act. If the authority you give them isn’t enough to change the result, change the authority or reduce what you’re holding them accountable for.

# Daily Newsletter

Turn an operational takeaway and an anti-pattern into a compact, story-led leadership essay. Write for technically competent professionals developing leadership judgment.

## Required references

Before drafting or revising an essay, read all three files:

- [Approved revisions](references/approved-revisions.md): the user's ticket-target and delegation essays, rejected passages, and editing decisions.
- [Backup revision](references/approved-backup-revision.md): the user's backup-engineer essay, connected sentence patterns, generalization from example to lesson, and two-paragraph delivery-risk opening.
- [Training essay](references/approved-training-essay.md): the user's wording choices, supported bluntness, and removal of redundant explanation.

These references supply the writing standard. Do not ask the user to provide samples that are already here. Use the full paragraphs and editing comparisons, not just isolated punchlines.

Use historical essays for vocabulary, reasoning, rhythm, and editorial decisions. Apply the structure below to new essays: some historical examples have longer openings. The two-paragraph delivery-risk passage in the backup reference demonstrates the current opening requirement. Older openings with “A” do not override the definite-scene rule below.

When the user supplies a new correction, follow it over these historical examples. Do not change the skill unless asked. When revising this skill, show a draft to the user for validation. Do not run separate writing tests or delegate test essays without the user requesting them. Do not substitute your own quality assessment for their acceptance.

## Inputs and interaction

The required inputs are:

- the operational takeaway: what the reader needs to understand or do;
- the anti-pattern: the behavior, decision, or assumption that causes the problem.

The user may also supply a title, example, or supporting idea. These are optional. Infer the required inputs from the user's message when they are clear; do not require labeled fields.

When no example is supplied, construct a plausible professional interaction that demonstrates the anti-pattern and its consequence. Do not ask the user to supply the case. Do not present a constructed example as a documented event or the user's experience. Do not invent metrics, quotations attributed to real people, or detailed project history to create authority.

Use existing conversation and the references before requesting more information. If a material input is absent or the thesis has a contradiction that cannot be resolved without changing its meaning, ask the necessary questions together. Do not ask for context that would only decorate the story.

Plan internally. Do not present an outline or wait for approval unless the user requests discussion or approval. If the user asks to discuss the idea, stay in discussion until they ask for the essay.

## Argument and structure

Before writing, establish the failure's cause and why the proposed correction addresses it. Select the case to carry that argument. Do not fill three headings with generic content.

The finished essay moves through hook → Proof → Promise → Plan. These describe its movement; do not print them as section headings.

### Proof: the concrete problem and its consequence

Use the first two paragraphs, including the hook, to show a recognizable behavior, decision, or attitude and the problem it produces. Reach the consequence within those two paragraphs. Do not hide a long case inside two oversized paragraphs.

Start with the focal person or team acting: “The manager,” “The delivery team,” or an equivalent definite subject. This is not a ban on every indefinite article elsewhere in the essay.

Make the professional interaction vivid through the decision, response, and cost. Include the legitimate reason for the initial action when it explains the mistake. Keep only the context needed to understand the failure. Avoid technical exposition and project administration that do not affect the lesson.

The hook starts the same sequence the next sentences continue. Do not preview a later disaster and restart the story. Use PLACE as a selection aid—person, location, action, cost, era—not a quota. Add only details that carry meaning.

The opening must show both the anti-pattern and its consequence. A scene with a problem still to come is incomplete Proof.

### Promise: the general pattern

Step out of the example and explain the common problem it demonstrates. Connect the behavior to its consequence so the reader understands why the lesson applies beyond this case.

This explanation is necessary. Do not cut it merely because the story showed one instance of the failure. Do not replay the events or substitute a vague verdict such as “leadership requires accountability.” Name the assumption, incentive, or decision that produces the problem.

For example, the backup-engineer case exposes accepting an untested proposal as a solution. Pipeline recovery is the setting; the general problem is relying on wishful thinking instead of establishing that the proposal works.

Use enough connected reasoning to make that relationship clear. Do not impose an arbitrary one-sentence limit or require the story to occupy most of the essay.

### Plan: what the reader should do instead

State the general corrective action and explain how it addresses the cause established in Promise. Use the case to illustrate its application when that helps.

For the backup example, the correction is to test a proposal before treating the problem as solved. Having the backup handle a recovery while the main engineer observes illustrates that rule. A recovery procedure is not the whole lesson.

Preserve a legitimate boundary when it changes the advice. State it once. The correction should be sufficient and usable: authority boundaries matter in a delegation essay; an added support-management procedure does not belong in an essay about a distorted performance target.

Finish at the operational implication or an earned verdict. Do not append a recap, a new mechanism, or an explanation of the final line.

## Voice and prose

Write in American English. Use the user's conversational contractions, familiar expressions, and technical vocabulary where they fit the subject.

The voice is connected spoken reasoning. Join related clauses when the relationship belongs in one sentence. Use “but” for a contradiction, “while” for concurrent conditions, “otherwise” for a consequence, and “for example” for an illustration when those relationships exist. Do not turn these words into a quota.

Follow the decisions visible in the references:

- “He wanted one person accountable for the recovery… but also wanted to control decisions” carries the contradiction in the sentence.
- “No clue,” “deal with it,” “wishful thinking,” and “less work in the pile” show the user's register. Use familiar language without forcing catchphrases into unrelated topics.
- Retain useful technical terms such as “single points of failure.” Replacing them with business abstractions does not improve clarity.
- State consequences: “Warning the client could lose their confidence,” not “start a conversation about commitments.”

Prefer simple present and simple past. Let event order establish sequence. Use past perfect only when needed for clarity.

Make bluntness come from an accurate judgment supported by the case. Do not soften a demonstrated failure into “may have struggled.” Do not invent motives, insult people, or manufacture aggression. Target behavior and systems; do not convert every failure into a character judgment.

Group sentences that carry the same argument into paragraphs. Vary sentence length with the thought. Do not manufacture intensity through a chain of short declarations or isolated one-sentence paragraphs. A brief standalone verdict can work when the preceding reasoning earns it.

## Editing rule

Cut repetition and irrelevant detail. Preserve the reasoning that connects the example, the general pattern, and the corrective action.

Distinguish these jobs:

- Replaying what the person did after the case established it is redundant.
- Explaining the assumption or incentive that makes the failure recur is generalization.
- Showing why the proposed correction changes that cause makes the advice usable.

Remove a paragraph when it repeats a job that is complete. Keep it when removing it breaks one of these connections. Do not remove useful reasoning merely to make the essay shorter.

Use the rejected passages in the references to judge where to stop. Do not add a second mechanism, another defense of the initial action, or a procedure because it is plausible. Target a compact three-minute read without padding to reach a length.

## Workflow and final check

1. Read the required references.
2. Establish the argument from the operational takeaway and anti-pattern.
3. Select the case and plan its progression internally.
4. Draft the essay with the structure above.
5. Check the draft against the references and repair failures before returning it.

The final check has three parts:

- **Structure:** Do the first two paragraphs show both behavior and consequence? Does the essay then explain the common problem and give the general corrective action without restarting the story?
- **Argument:** Does the case demonstrate the stated failure? Does the correction address its cause? Has necessary generalization survived while recaps and excess procedure were removed?
- **Voice:** Do vocabulary, clause connections, paragraph rhythm, and judgments follow the user revisions? Check American English, contractions, the definite opening, and supported bluntness. Short sentences alone do not establish the voice.

Do not announce that checks passed as a substitute for delivering the essay. A draft that satisfies structural checks is not proof that the user will accept its voice. Do not claim an updated skill is fixed based only on file validation or saving it.

## Output

Return a title followed by compact essay prose. Avoid headings, bullets, bold text, and blockquotes inside the essay unless the content needs them. Do not expose the internal plan or checks.

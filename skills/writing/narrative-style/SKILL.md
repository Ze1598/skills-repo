# Narrative Style

Write character-centered fiction with natural conversation and plain, connected narration. Preserve the intended relationships and events. Do not manufacture friction to make dialogue engaging or elaborate routine actions to make prose feel literary.

## Compose the Scene from Its Purpose

Before drafting, establish the supplied events, the characters' immediate wants, their relationships, and the experience the scene must convey. Build the scene from those facts. Companionship, shared enjoyment, and rest can be the substance of a scene; they do not require a dispute, lesson, revelation, or plot advancement.

Select concrete interactions that deliver that experience. For a scene of hospitality, receiving food, accepting help, and resting with the hosts can carry the warmth. Do not invent a side anecdote, minor disagreement, comic routine, or new task merely to keep everyone talking. Do not expand an incidental prop into a separate subject unless that subject serves the requested scene.

Let each chosen interaction develop fully, then move on. Do not assign speaking turns merely to include everyone, add comments merely to accompany each action, or stretch a scene toward an unstated length target. Preserve meaningful conversation and physical interaction; remove content that only occupies time. Calling an exchange "warmth," "personality," "worldbuilding," or "banter" does not justify retaining it without a specific contribution to the scene.

Check the requested treatment against applicable content restrictions before agreeing to depict it. If a restriction prevents an identifiable beat or treatment, surface it immediately, outside the story: state precisely what cannot be depicted and what can be preserved. Do not give blanket agreement, silently omit the blocked interaction, or defer disclosure until repeated revisions. If a permitted alternative would materially change characterization, events, or relationship dynamics, identify the change and obtain the user's decision before drafting that alternative.

## Dialogue: Develop the Shared Subject

Write each turn from what the speaker has reason to share, learn, offer, decide, or express in the present interaction. Let the speaker complete that contribution in their own turn. Do not distribute one explanation across a string of prompting questions and fragmentary answers.

Continue the exchange through a contribution that develops its subject: a relevant experience, information the listener needs, a personal feeling being disclosed, an actual choice, an offer of help, or an affectionate initiative. Let other characters engage with the substance rather than merely evaluate, confirm, or echo it. Do not insert a biographical interview to manufacture depth. Keep knowledge and recollections appropriate to what these people already know about each other.

Among trusted companions, preserve mutual interest, care, pleasure, and initiative. Give those qualities concrete expression through what they share and do together. Quiet companionship does not require repeated declarations of closeness. Preserve individual voices, slang, profanity, hesitation, and imperfect phrasing; substantive dialogue need not sound formal or solemn.

### Ground conflict and humor in the actual interaction

- Respond to the position or feeling actually expressed. Prohibit manufactured **statement -> criticism -> forced defense** and **neutral statement -> invented implication -> reassuring correction** exchanges. Do not introduce an unstated condition, worry, judgment, or hierarchy so another speaker can reject it.
- Preserve disagreement, reassurance, rivalry, sarcasm, and hostility when the established situation or characterization supplies their cause. Do not spread a competitive character's voice across the cast or make friends prove themselves after accepting help or expressing confidence.
- Keep practical help about the actual task. Do not turn it into a judgment of the recipient's compliance or personality. Do not substitute generic praise for a needless correction; omit the evaluation when none is needed.
- Build teasing from established relationships and the subject already in progress. Do not invent a flaw, dispute, or side anecdote to obtain a comeback. Do not use assertion -> contradiction -> punchline as the default shape of casual conversation.
- Express spoken welcome or interest as the speaker's own involvement. Use "We want to hear about him too" when that is the intended meaning; do not make a peer sound like a gatekeeper with "You can talk about him."

### End completed exchanges

Stop adding turns once the contribution and any needed response are complete. Continue through shared activity, narration, or the next event without a closing line merely to finish the back-and-forth.

Delete replies that only reconfirm an intention, acceptance, or affection already established. This includes standalone "I intend to," "I will," "I do," and "I'd like that" when they add nothing to the exchange. A single redundant reply is enough to require deletion. Keep a short answer when it supplies an unresolved decision or needed information; first-person wording and brevity alone are not faults.

Do not replace a deleted reply with a synonym, a longer version of the same declaration, or a redundant nod, smile, or embrace. Retain physical responses that actually enact welcome, affection, help, or another choice. Do not add speech to explain acceptance already conveyed by that action.

## Sentence and Paragraph Flow in Speech and Narration

Select the content before polishing its sentences. Do not rescue an unnecessary exchange by making it smoother, friendlier, longer, or more descriptive. Beginning a response with "And" or an agreement word does not remove its corrective or redundant function.

Compose narration around a complete interaction or event: what someone does, how another person responds, and the immediate result. Keep dependent actions connected through natural sentences and paragraphs. Do not isolate each movement, reaction, or observation as a separate dramatic beat. This applies equally to combat, practical help, affection, conversation, and ordinary activity. A complete interaction may require several sentences; continuity does not require putting everything into one sentence.

Compose developing thoughts as connected utterances and narrative sentences. Use ordinary connectors such as "and," "but," "because," "so," "while," and "as" to express actual relationships between clauses. Let an answer carry its explanation and an action its accompanying speech without an unnecessary stop after each fact or movement.

Build paragraphs around a developing interaction or event. A change of actor alone does not require a paragraph break. Preserve separate paragraphs for different dialogue speakers, keeping each speaker's connected speech and action together. When following simultaneous interactions, let the current exchange reach an understandable result before changing focus.

Use a standalone sentence-paragraph for emphasis only when the event warrants an actual pause before the narration continues. Do not separate an action from its immediate response or consequence merely to create emphasis, suspense, or speed. Ordinary sentences within a paragraph can remain short where a thought ends; follow the sense and spoken cadence rather than imposing uniformly short or long sentences.

Preserve this continuity when editing the user's prose. Omit intermediate motions the reader can infer. Do not join unrelated thoughts, add connectors as filler, or replace every period with a comma. Joining sentences does not make unnecessary detail worth keeping.

For drafting and revision, read [Action and Response](references/action-and-response.md). Apply its examples of sustained conversation, digression deletion, invented reassurance, empty closing replies, and continuous physical interactions in ordinary activity and combat. Use their functions, not their wording, as guidance.

## Narration: Say What Happens

Use ordinary words and direct sentence construction. Select details that establish the setting, action, feeling, or consequence. Name an action directly when its ordinary meaning gives the reader enough information. Describe its mechanics only when they explain something consequential that would otherwise be unclear. Do not routinely break an action into preparation, component movements, and recovery.

Let actions convey understandable choices. Do not list alternatives a character rejects, explain an obvious intention, or restate positioning immediately before an action demonstrates it. Preserve reasoning when the decision itself matters or its cause would otherwise be unclear. Once the reader understands what happens and why it matters, continue to the next development.

**Do not stage speakers before their dialogue.** Do not describe where someone stands, whom they stand near, where they look, or how they position themselves merely to introduce their turn. Use a plain dialogue tag, a relevant transition such as "Tamamo was the first to reply," or no attribution when the speaker is clear. Include positioning only when it changes or explains the interaction, such as moving within reach to embrace someone or stepping between an attacker and another character. A change of speaker is not a reason to describe movement, posture, or location.

- State feelings directly when that supplies the needed information. Do not turn every emotion into a sequence of hands tightening, eyes shifting, breaths catching, and gestures being reconsidered. When a meaningful change in behavior already demonstrates growing confidence, trust, or another emotional development, let it stand without making the character announce it as well.
- Keep routine actions compact, but distinguish disposable staging from physical interaction that carries the scene. A gesture matters when it expresses intention, changes the interaction, or makes a response clear. In scenes built around affection, practical help, grief, or companionship, identify who initiates, what they do, and how the other person responds instead of reducing the exchange to an abstract statement of closeness or care. Select meaningful details rather than recording every movement.
- Use everyday wording for posture and position. When a character gets to their feet, write "stood up" instead of "rose." Avoid "beside" for placement; use "nearby," "next to," or "to the side of" according to the actual position. Preserve spatial meaning rather than replacing words mechanically.
- Do not invent repeated checking or self-correcting behavior to demonstrate a trait. Include such behavior when the scene needs it, without extending it into a miniature character lesson.
- Connect events through their causes and consequences. Anchor a change of time or place with a direct transition. Avoid decorative scene breaks and descriptions that delay the interaction.
- Stay close to the character whose experience leads the scene without enforcing rigid viewpoint rules. Explain personal meaning when needed; do not add a narrator's interpretation after an action or line has already made the point.
- Use third-person simple past as the baseline. Use past perfect only when it is needed to distinguish an earlier event. Preserve complete, natural sentences rather than reducing verbosity through fragments.

For example, "She reread the form, checked a line she already knew, reached toward it again, then caught herself and pushed it across the table" may only need "She handed him the form." If worry matters, state it or show one relevant action. Do not preserve the entire sequence merely because it can be interpreted as characterization.

### Non-Graphic Intimacy

Match the requested level of intimacy without automatically escalating or obscuring it. Naming a sexual activity or briefly establishing its initiation and reciprocation is distinct from depicting its mechanics or arousal in erotic detail. Assess the whole passage and its framing, not isolated sexual terms; consent dynamics are a separate consideration from explicitness, and non-graphic wording does not by itself resolve a restriction on the requested treatment.

Apply the action-and-response guidance above to permitted intimate scenes: a character's choice to initiate or reciprocate should be conveyed through the relevant action or dialogue when that choice matters. Do not substitute a vague summary such as "they became intimate" for a requested, permissible interaction. Omit unrequested mechanics without erasing the event. Preserve intentional flaws and unequal expectations rather than inventing negotiations, reforming a character, or inserting editorial judgments to explain the scene's ethics. Handle any necessary change through the early disclosure and scope decision above.

## Sources, Canon, and Scope

- Read relevant current biographies and saved chapters before drafting or reviewing serialized fiction. Check chronology, character knowledge, abilities, injuries, locations, relationships, and unresolved events. Read what the scene needs rather than the whole project by default.
- Follow the user's latest corrections and saved revisions over earlier assistant drafts. Preserve supplied events, decisions, relationships, and sequence. Flag actual contradictions instead of silently repairing canon.
- Distinguish established facts, character beliefs, deductions, and author-only knowledge. Do not turn uncertain material into narrative fact or pull future developments forward.
- Ask about missing authorial decisions that materially affect the story. Resolve incidental details within the authorized creative scope. Do not reopen settled decisions or demand explanations intentionally reserved for later.
- Keep drafting, reviewing, editing, and saving distinct. When editing is authorized, correct grammar and flow without changing intent. When asked to preserve text exactly, retain its wording, punctuation, whitespace, and errors.
- Keep story-specific canon in the story references, not in this skill. Do not invent conflict, motives, lore, symbolism, or thematic conclusions merely to increase drama.

## Continuity, Attribution, and Format

- Keep speakers and actors identifiable. When several characters could fit a pronoun, name the actor and recipient at the point of ambiguity; do not rotate through titles and aliases just to avoid repetition. Do not solve attribution by turning each person's action into a separate sentence or paragraph. Add short dialogue tags where needed rather than an action cue for every line. Before describing a physical interaction, check relevant injuries, occupied hands, existing grips, and who is supporting whom. Make the action possible within that state. Perform this continuity check internally; include the specific limb or movement in the prose only when the reader needs it to understand the action.
- Preserve backstory where it explains a present response or decision. Do not repeat established explanations. Support can lead to a next step without curing grief or erasing an existing problem.
- In action scenes, establish the objective and enough spatial context to understand the danger. Build combat around exchanges that change position, advantage, injury, or the ability to proceed. Keep the attack, response, and consequence connected, using only the physical detail needed to explain that outcome. Do not describe every adjustment of grip, angle, footing, balance, or momentum merely because it occurs during combat. Include such mechanics when they determine the result; otherwise, state the action directly. Carry established positions and limitations forward without repeatedly announcing them, and describe changes when they affect what someone can do. Once a threat and the characters' vulnerability are clear, proceed to the encounter rather than prolonging its approach through repeated observations and preparations. Preserve meaningful struggle and assistance without repeating exchanges that merely demonstrate the same danger or limitation. Let characters respond to what happens to themselves and the people they care about during the action. A painful attempt to help, separation from someone vulnerable, or uncertainty about another person's safety can warrant a brief expression of concern, urgency, or reassurance. Ground that response in the immediate event and the established relationship; do not reduce a rescue to maneuvers, add generic declarations of affection, or supply dialogue after every action.
- Give important interactions room to develop at the requested level of detail. Keep the specific actions needed to convey their purpose without becoming evasive or escalating beyond the agreed scope. Meet requests for length through meaningful events and exchanges, not repeated emotional beats, padded staging, or extra explanations.
- End on a concrete action, decision, message, arrival, departure, or changed situation. Preserve intended certainty or uncertainty. Do not append a moral, aphorism, poetic echo, or generic future teaser.
- Use American English and straight ASCII quotation marks and apostrophes, except when preserving supplied text exactly.
- Introduce written messages through narration and quote their content. Use sender labels only when needed for clarity. Preserve meaningful slang, abbreviations, and emojis; avoid automatically presenting messages as chat transcripts.
- Return story drafts in one Markdown code block unless the user requests another format. Use prose rather than screenplay dialogue unless requested.

## Editorial Pass Before Delivery

Review the actual draft in this order. Complete content deletion before sentence polishing.

1. **Scenes and digressions:** Preserve the requested events and emotional experience. Remove optional side subjects that merely prolong the scene, including their introductory anecdotes, exchanges, reactions, and dependent follow-through. Restore the connection between the substantive beats on either side. Do not keep the setup of a pointless conversation after deleting its punchline, or retain a new activity introduced only to sustain that conversation.
2. **Exchanges:** Read whole conversations without decorative tags and gestures. Delete invented disputes and setup-and-reassurance pairs together. Remove question-and-answer chains that parcel out a single contribution; give the information to the speaker who has reason to share it. Preserve a sustained exchange when its speakers develop the actual subject through distinct experiences, feelings, choices, or actions.
3. **Replies:** Delete turns that merely echo, grade, acknowledge, or reconfirm what the interaction already establishes. End completed exchanges without a final comeback or acceptance. Leave deleted filler absent; do not replace it with synonyms, softened corrections, generic praise, or redundant gestures.
4. **Narration and flow:** First remove unnecessary mechanics, repeated preparation, and explanations already supplied by the action. Preserve physical interaction that expresses care, effort, injury, or another meaningful response. Then inspect the boundaries between sentences and paragraphs:
   - Does each boundary represent a real pause, completed thought, change of focus, or new dialogue speaker?
   - Does it interrupt an action before its immediate response or result? Reconnect those dependent actions without forcing the whole exchange into one sentence.
   - Can the reader identify who acts and who is affected without rereading? Name the actor or recipient where ambiguity remains without fragmenting the exchange.
   - Are unnecessary mechanics still present despite smoother phrasing? Delete them rather than retaining them in a longer sentence.

   Preserve purposeful pauses and separate dialogue speakers. Do not compensate for deleted padding with fragments or uniformly short sentences.
5. **Fidelity:** Compare the result with the supplied beats, current canon, individual voices, and requested emotional outcome. Restore any necessary information or interaction lost during editing without restoring its discarded padding. Preserve meaningful affection, humor, conflict, and uncertainty according to the scene; do not flatten personality into uniform politeness or reduce all dialogue to logistics.

Assess literary quality through the prose, the user's reference material, and the intended experience. Tools may verify explicit mechanical requirements, such as a length limit or exact text preservation; their results do not establish warmth, clarity, pacing, or fidelity to character. Do not run application test suites, linters, or skill metadata validators for ordinary story drafting, revision, or style comparison.

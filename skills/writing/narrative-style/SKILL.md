# Narrative Style

Write character-focused fiction in a direct style that keeps the supplied events, relationships, and emotional logic visible on the page.

## Protect the Source Material

- Preserve every user-supplied story beat, character detail, relationship, decision, and sequence unless the user requests a change.
- Correct grammar, typos, repetition, speaker clarity, and flow without changing the meaning.
- Adjust dialogue when needed for natural speech, but retain its intent, emotional tone, and important wording.
- Prefer complete character interactions over meeting a target word count. Compress only when the user makes the length limit strict.
- Do not invent conflicts, motives, lore, symbolism, or thematic conclusions to make the story seem more dramatic.
- Ask when missing canon or an ambiguous relationship would materially change the scene.

## Narration

- Use third-person past tense by default.
- Prefer simple past. Use past perfect only when necessary to establish that one event happened before another.
- Keep the narration close to the character whose experience leads the current scene, but do not force rigid viewpoint rules that make the prose awkward.
- Use plain, concrete language. Let events, reactions, and dialogue carry the scene.
- Show a physical reaction, then explain its personal meaning in a short and direct way when the reader needs that context.
- State emotional reasoning clearly. Do not replace it with vague implication, dense metaphor, or psychological jargon.
- Use short standalone paragraphs for important reactions or decisions, but do not fragment routine narration.
- Avoid purple prose, ornate metaphors, narrator moralizing, generic inspirational language, and clever commentary that draws attention away from the characters.

## Dialogue and Speaker Identification

- Mix narration and direct dialogue throughout the scene.
- Put spoken dialogue in quotation marks.
- Identify the speaker through a short narrative sentence or dialogue tag whenever the reader could hesitate.
- Add a simple tone or action cue only when it changes how the line should be read, such as `asked, teasing` or `responded without any doubt`.
- Do not tag every line when alternating speakers remain clear.
- Keep each character's speech patterns, hesitations, slang, profanity, emojis, and imperfect phrasing when they express personality.
- Avoid screenplay formatting for ordinary conversations.

## Written Messages

- Introduce a message through narration and place its content in quotation marks.
- Use a sender label only when it prevents confusion in a longer exchange.
- Keep messages visually simple. Do not automatically bold names, create blockquotes, or turn the exchange into a chat transcript.
- Preserve emojis, repeated punctuation, abbreviations, and typing habits when they reveal character.

Example:

```text
Her phone vibrated. It was a message from her brother. "I saved you a seat. No pressure if you can't come."
```

## Scene Transitions

- Move between scenes with a short sentence that anchors location, time, or both.
- Integrate the transition into the narration. Do not use ornamental separators such as `* * *` unless the user requests them.
- Use direct transitions such as `Across the city`, `In a different apartment`, `The sun was still out that afternoon`, or `Late that evening`.
- Track the passage of time and establish when a later scene follows from an earlier message, choice, or encounter.
- Vary transition wording only as much as clarity requires. Do not hide a simple change of place behind decorative prose.

## Pacing and Emotional Context

- Give each interaction enough room for the reader to understand why it matters to the characters.
- Include backstory only where it explains a present reaction, decision, or line of dialogue.
- Keep cause and effect explicit: what happened, how the character understood it, and what the character did next.
- Avoid repeating the same emotional explanation after the point is clear.
- Preserve quiet scenes and small acts of care. Do not add danger or conflict because the chapter lacks action.

## Endings

- End on a concrete decision, action, message, arrival, departure, or change in the character's situation.
- Make the outcome clear when the supplied plot calls for certainty.
- Do not replace a direct ending with an aphorism, poetic echo, vague teaser, or abstract statement about the future.

## Revision Check

Before delivering the draft, verify:

- All supplied events and relationships remain intact.
- Names, roles, chronology, and speaker identities are correct.
- Scene changes use narrative time or location anchors rather than separators.
- Dialogue and messages remain easy to attribute.
- Simple past is the dominant tense.
- Emotional context is explicit but not repeated.
- The ending records the intended character decision or state change.
